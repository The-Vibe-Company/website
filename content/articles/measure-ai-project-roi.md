You calculate the ROI of an AI project like any other investment: measured gains, minus the full cost, divided by the full cost. The hard part is elsewhere. You need to know what to measure, and to measure it before launch. Without a baseline, no "after" number proves anything.

:::summary{title="In short"}
Track five metrics, defined before launch and recorded every week.
- **Time per case**: how many minutes the work takes, before and after.
- **Usage rate**: the share of the AI's output that actually gets used.
- **Correction rate**: the share a human rewrites or rejects.
- **Full cost per case**: model, infrastructure and review time included.
- **Business outcome**: the lead time, revenue or risk the process is meant to move.
ROI and payback period are calculated from these five numbers.
:::

## Why so few companies measure the ROI of their AI

According to [KPMG's Global AI Pulse](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/xx/pdf/2026/04/global-ai-pulse.pdf), a survey of 2,110 senior executives in 20 countries published in May 2026, 95% of organizations surveyed have an AI strategy and 64% report meaningful business value. Only 8% report established return on investment.

Other surveys point the same way. In [IBM's 2025 CEO Study](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2025-05-06-ibm-study-ceos-double-down-on-ai-while-navigating-enterprise-hurdles) of 2,000 CEOs, only 25% of AI initiatives delivered the expected ROI, and 16% scaled enterprise-wide. In [McKinsey's State of AI 2025](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-2025), 39% of respondents attribute any EBIT impact to AI. Most of them put it under 5% of EBIT.

In France, measurement is even harder. [KPMG](https://kpmg.com/fr/fr/media/press-releases/2026/05/ai-pulse-survey-2026.html) found that 50% of French companies struggle to measure the indirect benefits of AI, against 44% worldwide.

These numbers mostly describe a method problem. Most projects start without a starting number, count licences instead of results, and forget the time humans spend reviewing. One figure also travels a lot: "95% of AI projects fail". It comes from a preliminary MIT report (Project NANDA) whose [method is widely questioned](https://www.theregister.com/software/2025/08/18/generative-ai-does-nothing-for-95-percent-of-companies/1118115). Don't build a business case on it.

## Before launch: the baseline

You record the baseline on the current process, before the AI touches it. Three numbers are enough to start:

1. **Volume**: how many cases per week (tickets, files, documents, quotes).
2. **Time per case**: how many minutes a person spends on one, start to finish.
3. **Error rate**: the share of cases reworked, corrected or disputed afterwards.

Record them over a few representative weeks, with the people who do the work. This is also when you write down the number that triggers what comes next: above which level you widen the scope, below which you stop.

This step fits on one page. It makes the difference when you defend the budget for the second project.

## The 5 metrics of AI project ROI

![The 5 metrics of AI project ROI, from the baseline to the ROI calculation. Detailed in the text.](/images/resources/roi-ia/ai-roi-5-metrics.png)

### 1. Time per case

Time per case measures how many minutes the work takes with AI, compared with the baseline. Record it on the same scope, with the same people. The gross time saved is: (time before − time after) × volume.

Count the time end to end. If the AI drafts in ten seconds but someone then spends several minutes checking, the total is what counts.

### 2. Usage rate

Usage rate measures the share of the AI's output that actually gets used: drafts sent, alerts handled, documents kept. It's the most honest metric, because it doesn't depend on anyone's opinion.

At [LocService](/en/case-studies/locservice), a peer-to-peer rental platform that gets hundreds of tickets a day, the day-to-day measure is simple: is the draft used? Drafts generated by Quivr's autodraft are used on 87% of tickets. A human reviews and sends every reply.

A low usage rate often means the AI lacks context. The details are in our article on [AI agents for customer support](/en/resources/articles/ai-customer-support-agent).

### 3. Correction rate

Correction rate measures the share of the AI's work that a human rewrites, corrects or rejects. It completes the usage rate: a draft that gets used but half rewritten doesn't save much.

For document checks, quality also shows in what the AI finds. For [Coup de Pates](/en/case-studies/coup-de-pates), three skills compare, check and cross-check product documents. Across two versions of one specification, the AI flagged 72 changes. The quality team decides on each one.

### 4. Full cost per case

Full cost per case adds up everything it costs to handle one case with AI:

- model calls (tokens or subscription, brought down to one case);
- infrastructure and hosting;
- human review time;
- maintenance: prompt or skill updates, monitoring, incidents.

Review time is the line people forget most. On its own it can wipe out the time saved in metric 1. Keep the upfront investment (scoping, development, integration) separate: you need it for the payback period.

### 5. Business outcome

Business outcome measures what the process should change for the company: a shorter response time, more files handled with the same team, fewer costly errors, a risk better covered. Pick one main outcome per project, one that leadership already tracks.

This is the metric that ties the project to the P&L. [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-2025) notes that the companies getting the most value from AI redesign their workflows far more often than others. Time saved that changes nothing in the process often stays invisible in the accounts.

## Calculate ROI and payback period

With these five numbers, the maths fits in two formulas:

- **ROI** = (measured gains − full cost) / full cost, over a given period (often 12 months).
- **Payback period** = upfront investment / net monthly gain.

Measured gains combine time saved (metric 1, valued at the real hourly cost), errors avoided and the business outcome when it can be priced. Full cost comes from metric 4, plus the upfront investment spread over the period.

| Line | What goes in | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time saved | (time before − time after) × volume × hourly cost | Metric 1 and baseline |
| Errors avoided | drop in error rate × volume × cost of one error | Metric 3 and baseline |
| Business outcome | priced gain on the main outcome, if measurable | Metric 5 |
| Running cost | full cost per case × volume | Metric 4 |
| Upfront investment | scoping, development, integration | Project budget |

Keep the assumptions visible in the table. Anyone who disputes them should be able to change one number and watch the result move. ## The traps that skew ROI

- **Measuring adoption instead of results.** A tool the whole team opens every day can leave the process unchanged.
- **Forgetting review time.** It's often the first real cost line.
- **Measuring too early.** The first weeks mix learning and tuning. Compare stable weeks.
- **Crediting AI with the whole gain.** If the process changed at the same time, say so.
- **Changing scope along the way.** Compare the same volume, the same kind of case, the same teams.

## Decide: stop, continue or widen

ROI is there to support a decision. According to [Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027), over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, because of rising costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls. A rule written before launch keeps you out of that scenario.

- **Stop** if the usage rate stays below the threshold you set after the run-in period, or if full cost exceeds the gain.
- **Continue as is** if ROI is positive but the correction rate stays high: improve the context given to the AI first.
- **Widen** (more volume, more autonomy) when all five metrics hold for several weeks in a row.

## FAQ

### How do you calculate the ROI of an AI project?

Divide the net gain (measured gains minus full cost) by the full cost, over a given period. Gains come from time saved, errors avoided and the business outcome, compared with a baseline recorded before launch. Full cost includes the model, infrastructure, maintenance and human review time.

### Which KPIs should you track for an AI project?

Five are enough to start: time per case, usage rate, correction rate, full cost per case and one main business outcome. Record them every week, on the same scope as the baseline.

### How long does it take to measure AI ROI?

Long enough to record a baseline over a few representative weeks, then to compare stable weeks after the run-in period. Measuring during tuning gives misleading numbers, in both directions.

### Why do so few companies report measurable AI ROI?

According to KPMG, only 8% of organizations surveyed in 2026 report established ROI. The usual causes: no baseline, adoption metrics mistaken for results, and hidden costs such as human review.

### Does the first AI project need a positive ROI?

It needs reliable numbers at least. A first project that is well measured, even a modest one, tells you what to do next. A project without measurement lets you neither stop nor widen.
